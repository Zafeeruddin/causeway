A road to cameras you cannot otherwise reach.

Cameras on a customer's private network sit behind a VPN, then behind a jump host, then on an address only that jump host can route to. Causeway walks that chain, names the hop that failed when one does, and puts the picture in a browser.

VPN dial→ certificate trust→ whitelist→ route to jump host→ SSH auth→ port forward→ camera reachable→ stream handshake
The ladder

Eight gates, walked in order, each reporting for itself.

The usual answer to a camera that will not play is “it isn't working”, with no indication of which of five hops broke. Causeway makes the chain a first-class thing. A hop a profile does not use is shown as skipped, not hidden, so not needed never looks like never checked.

What it does

Four jobs, and the third one is the hard one.

Connects

Fortinet, GlobalProtect, WireGuard, or nothing at all when the cameras are already reachable. One network namespace per profile, so three teams hold three VPNs on one host and a dropped tunnel takes down its own traffic and nobody else's.

Watches

Live preview in the browser over WebRTC, about five seconds from click to picture. Two people watching one camera cost one camera session, because cameras cap those hard and the second viewer is the one who gets refused.

Records

The raw feed and the inferred feed at once, into sealed segments that survive a mid-write outage, uploaded to S3-compatible storage. A recording that lost thirty seconds says so, and says why, in a sidecar that travels with the file.

Compares

Both feeds side by side on one transport, aligned on wall-clock time, so a gap in one does not silently shift the other and make two synchronised cameras look like two different afternoons.

Three ideas

The decisions the rest of it rests on.

The kill switch is a routing fact, not a policy.

Inside a profile's namespace the default route belongs to the tunnel. When the VPN drops there is no route for camera traffic to fall back to, so it fails instead of quietly leaving by the host's normal egress and reaching the internet from an address the customer never approved. Control traffic reaches Postgres and Redis over a more specific route that survives the default being replaced.

Segments are MPEG-TS, not MP4.

An MP4 becomes readable when its moov atom is written, which happens when the muxer exits cleanly — exactly what does not happen when a tunnel dies mid-write. Whatever reached the disk as TS plays. The session is concatenated to MP4 at the end, where there is a clean exit to rely on.

A failure names the hop that failed.

Not “connection error”. The database password, the missing kernel module, the camera that authenticated and then refused the stream — each arrives as the sentence an operator can act on. Most of the tests in this codebase exist to keep it that way.

What it costs

Measured on the machine it shipped to, not estimated.

A configured camera costs nothing. Cost arrives when somebody watches or records, which means the sizing question is how many people at once, not how many cameras.

H.265 previews are the only expensive line here, because no mainstream browser decodes H.265 over WebRTC. Those and only those are re-encoded — on an NVIDIA card when there is one, with frames staying on the card between decode and encode. Past its budget the machine refuses a stream rather than admitting it and juddering across every stream at once, which is the failure that reads like a network problem and is not one.
